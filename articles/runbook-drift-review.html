Runbook Drift Review After Production Incidents

By James ChenPublished June 23, 20268 min read

A runbook is useful until the system changes and nobody tells the runbook. After a few incidents, the document can become a museum of old commands, retired dashboards, and escalation paths that only worked two team reorganizations ago.

I like reviewing runbook drift immediately after the incident timeline is stable, but before the retro energy disappears. The goal is not to rewrite every page. The goal is to catch the places where responders had to improvise because the documented path did not match production anymore.

Start with the moments people left the runbook

In the incident notes, mark every point where responders stopped following the documented process. Sometimes that is healthy judgment. Sometimes it means the alert named the wrong service, the dashboard link was stale, or the mitigation step assumed infrastructure that no longer exists.

Separate missing context from wrong instructions

Missing context means the step was technically right but not enough: no explanation of expected latency, no owner for a dependency, no warning that a restart causes cache warmup pain. Wrong instructions are more dangerous: commands that no longer work, rollback steps that skip data migration, or contacts who are no longer on call.

Assign one owner per stale section

Runbook cleanup fails when the retro action says "update docs." Name the page, the stale section, and the owner. If three teams share the runbook, pick one person to coordinate the change and ask the other teams to review only the parts they own.

Keep the edit close to the incident

The best runbook details are still fresh in the first few days: which graph finally explained the issue, which command was safe, which customer-impact phrase worked, and which mitigation sounded good but made the queue worse. Waiting a month turns those details into polite guesses.

Test the updated path lightly

Do a small dry run after editing. Open the links, verify the dashboard loads, check that command examples use current names, and ask one engineer who was not in the incident to read the new path. If they cannot follow the first five minutes, the update is not finished.
