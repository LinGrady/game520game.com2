Understanding the EU AI Act: What Tech Companies Actually Need to Do

👤 Elena Vasquez 📅 March 15, 2026 ⏱️ 12 min read
Advertisement

I've spent the last eight months helping companies prepare for the EU AI Act. Most of them started with the same misconception: that this is a future problem, something to deal with in 2026 or 2027. The reality is much more immediate, and the preparation required is much more substantial than most realize.

Let me give you the practical breakdown that I wish someone had given me two years ago. This isn't a legal summary (you'll need lawyers for that). This is what I've learned helps companies actually become compliant.

The Timeline Is Tighter Than You Think

The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024. Here's the timeline that matters:

February 2025: Prohibitions on unacceptable risk AI systems took effect. This includes social scoring systems, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces (with exceptions), and AI that exploits vulnerable groups.

August 2025: Requirements for high-risk AI systems start applying. This is where most tech companies will feel the impact.

August 2026: General-purpose AI models with systemic risk face additional requirements.

So if you're building AI products and serving European customers, you're already in the window where compliance matters. And for many companies, building compliant systems takes 12-18 months of work.

Understanding the Risk Classification

The AI Act categorizes AI systems by risk level. Where your system falls determines what you must do. Here's my practical guide to the categories.

Prohibited (Don't Do This)

These are AI systems so harmful they're banned outright. Think government social scoring, manipulative AI, and real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces. If you're building any of these, stop and reassess your business model.

High-Risk (Most Enterprise AI)

This is where it gets complicated. High-risk includes AI systems used in critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, and democratic processes. The list is long and, frankly, somewhat ambiguous.

A recruitment screening tool? High-risk. A chatbot handling customer service? Probably not. A system that helps doctors diagnose patients? Definitely high-risk. An AI that decides your credit limit? High-risk.

Here's the practical test I use with clients: if your AI makes or significantly influences decisions about people's lives—employment, credit, healthcare, education—it's likely high-risk. When in doubt, assume high-risk and work backward if you can justify a lower classification.

Limited and Minimal Risk

Limited risk systems (like chatbots) have transparency requirements—you need to tell users they're interacting with AI. Minimal risk systems (like AI in video games) have basically no requirements beyond existing law.

What High-Risk Compliance Actually Requires

For most enterprise AI companies, the high-risk requirements are what matter. Here's what compliance actually involves.

Risk Management System

You need a documented process for identifying, analyzing, and evaluating risks throughout your AI system's lifecycle. Not a one-time assessment—an ongoing process.

In practice, this means having a risk register that covers your AI systems, updated regularly, with concrete mitigation steps for identified risks. I've seen companies build elaborate frameworks, but what actually works is simple: a living document that your team actually uses.

Data Governance

Your training data needs to meet certain standards. You must document the data's origin, collection methodology, and any biases or limitations. For personal data, you need clear legal bases for processing.

I've found this is where most companies underestimate the effort. Building a comprehensive data lineage system retroactively is painful. If you're starting fresh, design for this from the beginning.

Technical Documentation

For each high-risk AI system, you need documentation covering: the system's intended purpose, the training data and methodologies, the system architecture and algorithms, performance metrics, and known limitations.

This documentation must be kept current and provided to authorities upon request. Think of it as a technical spec sheet that explains everything about how your system works.

Transparency and Disclosure

Users must be informed that they're interacting with AI. This sounds simple, but gets complicated in practice. What exactly does "informed" mean? A disclaimer in a terms of service that no one reads? A prominent disclosure? The Act suggests it should be clear and visible.

Additionally, you may need to disclose confidence scores, provide explanations for individual decisions when requested, and ensure humans can override AI decisions.

Human Oversight

High-risk AI must include mechanisms for human oversight. This means humans should be able to understand what the AI is doing, detect when it's malfunctioning, and override its decisions when necessary.

In practice, this often means designing your AI's outputs to be interpretable, building review processes for consequential decisions, and ensuring there's always a human in the loop for critical decisions.

Accuracy and Robustness

Your system must perform consistently and be resilient against adversarial inputs and cyberattacks. This requires testing—not just for performance, but for security and edge cases.

Logging and Audit Trails

You need logs. Lots of logs. Every significant decision, every error, every edge case should be logged in a way that allows you to reconstruct what happened and demonstrate compliance to regulators.

I recommend building logging as a first-class feature, not an afterthought. The compliance overhead for well-designed logging is much lower than retrofitting it later.

The General-Purpose AI Provisions

If you're training foundation models (not just deploying them), additional requirements apply. These kicked in for the largest models in August 2025, with full requirements for all general-purpose AI models coming in August 2026.

The key requirements: maintain technical documentation, comply with copyright obligations (this is a big one that the industry is still figuring out), and for larger models, conduct adversarial testing and report incidents to the AI Office.

If you're building GPT-style models, this matters to you. The copyright issue especially is still evolving—there are active lawsuits and the compliance approaches aren't settled.

Getting Started: A Practical Roadmap

Here's the approach I've found works for companies starting from scratch.

Month 1-2: Inventory and Classification

Identify every AI system you have or are building. Classify each one by risk level. This sounds straightforward but often reveals systems people forgot existed or weren't thinking of as "AI" (like recommendation algorithms or automated pricing).

Month 3-4: Gap Analysis

For your high-risk systems, assess your current state against requirements. Where are the gaps? What's missing? Prioritize based on impact and effort.

Month 5-8: Implementation

Address the critical gaps first. Usually this means: documentation infrastructure, logging systems, human oversight mechanisms, and risk management processes.

Month 9-12: Validation and Refinement

Test your compliance measures. Conduct internal audits. Get external reviews where appropriate. Refine based on findings.

Most companies I've worked with need 12-18 months for a thorough implementation. Companies that started early are now in good shape. Companies that are just starting are behind.

What I've Learned About Regulatory Compliance

After helping dozens of companies through this, here are the insights I think actually matter.

Treat compliance as product work, not legal work. The companies that do this well treat AI compliance like they treat security—it's a feature of the product, built by engineers, not just lawyers writing policies.

Start before you think you need to. The companies that are least stressed about the AI Act are the ones that started two years ago. Everyone else is scrambling.

Document everything from day one. You will not be able to reconstruct why decisions were made months ago. Keep records as you go.

Build for the spirit of the law, not just the letter. The regulations are principles-based in many areas. Regulators will care about whether you took the requirements seriously, not just whether you have the right paperwork.

Get good legal counsel. This isn't legal advice, but genuinely—find lawyers who specialize in this and actually understand AI. The regulatory landscape is evolving and you need guidance that's current.

The EU AI Act is the most comprehensive AI regulation in the world. Whether you think it's the right approach or overreach, it's the reality companies operating in Europe must navigate. The good news: many of the requirements are just good engineering practices anyway. Building compliant AI is, in many ways, building better AI.
