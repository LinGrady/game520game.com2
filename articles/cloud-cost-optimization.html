How We Reduced Our Cloud Costs by 40%: A Detailed Case Study

👤 Michael Thompson 📅 March 18, 2026 ⏱️ 15 min read
Advertisement

Six months ago, our monthly AWS bill was $47,000. Today, it's $28,200. That's a 40% reduction that saved us $225,600 annually—all without reducing functionality or performance. In this article, I'll share exactly how we did it, including the mistakes we made and the tools that actually helped.

Where Our Money Was Going

Before we could optimize, we needed to understand our spending. Here's what our AWS bill looked like before we started:

  • EC2 Instances: 42% - We were running 15 production servers plus 8 staging environments 24/7
  • RDS (Database): 23% - Three production databases running on db.r5.4xlarge instances
  • S3 Storage: 12% - Approximately 50TB of data storage and transfers
  • Data Transfer: 8% - Outbound data transfer costs were bleeding us dry
  • Other Services: 15% - CloudFront, Route 53, Lambda, etc.

Step 1: Right-Sizing Our EC2 Instances

The biggest win came from right-sizing our EC2 instances. We were running instances that were far more powerful than what our workloads actually needed.

We used AWS Compute Optimizer to analyze our instance utilization over a 2-week period. The results were embarrassing—we were using on average only 15-20% of our CPU capacity and 25% of our memory.

What We Changed

  • Moved 8 of our 15 production servers from m5.4xlarge to m5.xlarge (saved $3,200/month)
  • Implemented auto-scaling for non-production environments (saved $2,100/month)
  • Reserved Instances for our baseline 10 production servers (saved $1,800/month)

Lessons Learned

Right-sizing isn't a one-time task. CPU and memory usage patterns change as your application grows. We now review our instance sizes monthly using AWS Cost Explorer's rightsizing recommendations.

Step 2: Database Optimization

Our RDS costs were out of control. We had three production databases running on over-provisioned instances, and we weren't taking advantage of many AWS RDS features.

What We Changed

  • Downgraded our primary read database from db.r5.4xlarge to db.r5.2xlarge (saved $1,400/month)
  • Implemented Read Replicas for our reporting workloads instead of running separate databases
  • Enabled Aurora Serverless for our dev/test environments (saved $900/month)
  • Reserved Instances for our production databases (saved $1,100/month)

Step 3: S3 and Data Transfer Optimization

We were storing everything in S3 with default settings, and our data transfer costs were killing us.

What We Changed

  • Implemented S3 Intelligent-Tiering for objects with unpredictable access patterns (saved $1,100/month)
  • Enabled S3 Transfer Acceleration for our file uploads
  • Moved static assets to CloudFront with proper caching (reduced data transfer by 60%)
  • Compressed all log files before storing them in S3 (reduced storage by 40%)

The Mistakes We Made

Not everything went smoothly. Here are the costly mistakes we made along the way:

Mistake 1: Going Too Aggressive on Savings

In our enthusiasm to cut costs, we downsized one of our database instances too much. Within a week, we started experiencing performance issues during peak hours. We had to revert the change and wait for a less busy period to optimize properly.

Mistake 2: Ignoring Dev Environments

We initially focused only on production costs. But our dev and staging environments were running 24/7, costing us $4,500/month. We implemented auto-scaling and scheduled scaling to only run these environments during working hours.

Mistake 3: Not Using Reserved Instances Properly

We bought Reserved Instances for the wrong instance types and had to sell them at a loss on the AWS Reserved Instance Marketplace. Now we analyze our usage patterns for at least 3 months before committing to reserved capacity.

Tools That Helped Us

  • AWS Cost Explorer: Essential for understanding where your money is going
  • AWS Compute Optimizer: Provides right-sizing recommendations based on actual usage
  • AWS Budgets: Set alerts when costs exceed thresholds
  • CloudHealth (now VMware Aria): Third-party tool for more detailed analysis
  • Dashbird: Serverless monitoring that helped us optimize Lambda costs

Key Takeaways

  1. Start with visibility: You can't optimize what you can't measure. Make sure you have detailed cost allocation tags set up from day one.
  2. Right-size first: Most companies are over-provisioned. Use AWS tools to identify undersized resources.
  3. Automate non-production: Dev and staging environments don't need to run 24/7. Use scheduled scaling.
  4. Commit gradually: Only buy Reserved Instances for stable, predictable workloads.
  5. Make it ongoing: Cloud optimization isn't a one-time project. Review monthly.

What's Next

We've now turned our attention to further optimization. Our next targets are:

  • Moving some batch processing workloads to Spot Instances (potential savings: $1,500/month)
  • Implementing a multi-account strategy to isolate costs better
  • Exploring Kubernetes cost optimization with Karpenter
